Ten copies of a sentence are still one copy

When every word stays on the page, a child can write a line ten times and still not be able to say it.

A neat page that could not speak

One of my students brought me a notebook page. The same line, "I want water.", was written ten times down the margin. The first line was careful. The last line was tired.

I covered the page and asked for the line. The pause was long. The pen had practiced, but the mouth had not.

This happens in every language. A child learning Spanish can copy a want-line ten times and still point at the cup. A child learning English can copy a polite thank-you and still freeze when someone holds a door. While every word stays in view, the child never has to build the sentence.

Why the tenth copy is the same as the first

Copying a line you can see is a tracing job. The order of the words is already solved. The child does not choose the verb or the ending. The child just keeps the pen moving so the row looks finished. That can train neat handwriting. It does not train the moment when the page is gone.

Memory research keeps finding the same thing. Once a person can recall something, another try at recalling it helps later memory more than another look at it. Karpicke and Roediger reported this in 2007. Reading the open line again is the look. Saying the line with the paper covered is the recall try.

People also remember what they had to produce better than what they only read. Slamecka and Graf showed this in 1978. A line that stays in view for ten copies is closer to reading. A covered line makes the child produce something small.

This does not mean a beginner should start from a blank page. Beginners still need one finished example first. Sweller and Cooper showed in 1985 that a worked example helps beginners more than a blank problem. Then the help should leave one step at a time. For a child, that step is one word. Water becomes milk, and the rest of the line stays the same.

Three minutes tonight

  1. Pick one line your child has already met, like "I want water." If they cannot read it yet, read it to them once. They still say it.
  2. Read it once together, out loud, with the model in view. Then put the pen down.
  3. Cover the line. Your child says the whole line. If it breaks, show it once and cover it again. One rescue only, because a third look turns the cover back into a copy.
  4. Change one word. Show the new line once, cover it, and let your child say it. Keep every other word still.
  5. Stop while it is still easy. Tomorrow, use the same line with one new word. Do not make the page longer, and do not add a new tense.

What this is not

This is not about pen versus keyboard, because a keyboard can copy a line ten times too. It is not a mixed grammar page, because tonight only one word moves. And it is not about talking more, because what matters is the covered try and one rescue if it breaks.

The hard part was never the tenth line. Will you cover the page tonight and let your child say one line?
