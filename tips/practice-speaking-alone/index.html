How to Practice Speaking a Language Alone: 7 Habits That Work

By Mr. Jay, an English teacher in Korea

Many learners say the same thing: “I can read and I can understand, but when I need to speak, nothing comes out.” The usual advice is “find a native speaker to talk to.” That helps, but it isn’t always possible, and it can be scary at the start. The good news is that a lot of speaking practice can happen alone. Here are seven habits that really work, for English or any other language.

1. Narrate your day out loud

While you cook, walk, or get ready, describe what you are doing: “Now I’m cutting onions. I’m putting them in the pan. It smells good.” It feels silly for about two days. After that it becomes a quick way to find the words you don’t know. Every time you get stuck, you’ve found something to look up later.

Try it: five minutes of narration during one daily routine, like making breakfast.

2. Shadow short audio clips

Shadowing means playing a short clip and speaking along with it, almost at the same time, copying the rhythm and sound. Use something slightly easy: a podcast for learners, a short video with subtitles, or a scene from a show you like. Don’t worry about every word. Copy the music of the sentence first.

Try it: one 30-second clip, five times in a row, once a day.

3. Record yourself, then listen once

Recording yourself is uncomfortable, but it works. Talk for one minute about a simple topic (your weekend, your job, a food you love), then listen back once. You will notice pauses, repeated words, and sounds you want to improve. Keep the recordings. In a month, listen to the first one and you’ll hear your own progress.

4. Answer the same question three times

Pick one everyday question, like “What do you do on weekends?” Answer it out loud. Then answer it again, a bit faster, and a third time with one new detail. This is how you build fluency: not by learning new words every day, but by making the words you already know come out quickly.

5. Learn phrases, not just words

When you speak, you don’t build sentences one word at a time. You use chunks: “Do you mind if…”, “I was going to…”, “The thing is…”. Collect five chunks a week from things you read or watch, and say each one out loud in three different sentences about your own life. Chunks are the fastest way to sound natural.

6. Think in the language for tiny moments

You don’t have to “think in English” all day. Start with tiny moments: when you look at the clock, say the time in your target language in your head. When you see a dog, describe it. When you decide what to eat, argue with yourself in the language. These little moments train your brain to reach for the new language first.

7. Use an AI or app as a practice partner, carefully

Voice chat with an AI tool can be a low-pressure warm-up. Ask it to speak slowly and to ask you simple questions. But remember: a tool is a helper, not a teacher. Don’t let it correct every sentence. Ask for just one correction at the end, so you keep talking instead of stopping all the time.

Why speaking alone works

Speaking is a physical skill as much as a mental one. Your mouth, your breathing, and your memory all have to work together fast. Solo practice gives you a lot of repetitions with zero pressure, so when you do talk to a real person, the words are closer to the surface.

The most important part is courage. Nobody feels “ready” to speak. People who improve are the ones who start before they feel ready, make mistakes, and keep going. Start with one habit from this list this week, not all seven.
