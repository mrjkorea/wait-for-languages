How Parents Can Help With English Homework (Without Correcting Everything)

By Mr. Jay, an English teacher in Korea

When your child brings home English homework, it’s natural to want to fix every mistake. You want them to do well, and red marks feel like help. But correcting everything often has the opposite effect: children stop taking risks, write shorter answers, and start to see English as a test they keep failing. Here is a calmer approach that supports learning without taking over.

Why correcting everything backfires

Language learning needs courage. A child who writes “Yesterday I goed to the park and see a big dog” is actually doing a lot right: past time, a full idea, real details. If the page comes back covered in corrections, the lesson they learn is “writing more means more mistakes.” The next time, they write “I went to the park.” Correct, but they stopped trying.

Teachers also need to see what a child can really do on their own. If a parent has fixed the homework, the teacher can’t see what to teach next.

1. Ask first: “What is this homework for?”

Some homework is practice, some is a check, and some is simply reading. Ask your child to explain the task to you in their own words. If they can’t, that’s the first thing to help with. Understanding the task matters more than a perfect answer.

2. Praise the idea before the spelling

Start with what they said, not how they said it. “Oh, you saw a big dog? What color was it?” This shows that communication comes first. It also often leads to a longer, richer answer, which is exactly what teachers want to see.

3. Choose one thing to correct

Pick one pattern, not ten errors. If the same mistake appears several times (like -ed on past verbs), point to one example and ask, “Can you find another one like this?” Letting the child find and fix the rest builds real understanding. Leave the other mistakes for another day. They will still be there, and that’s okay.

4. Use questions instead of answers

Instead of writing the correct word, try:

  • “Read this sentence out loud. Does it sound right to you?”
  • “Is this happening now or yesterday?”
  • “How many dogs? One or more?”

Children often catch their own mistakes when they read aloud. Self-correction sticks much better than a correction from someone else.

5. If your own English isn’t confident, that’s fine

You don’t need perfect English to help. Some of the best help is simple: a quiet place to work, a regular time, and interest in what your child is learning. You can say, “Teach me what you learned today.” Explaining something to a parent is powerful practice, and it doesn’t matter if you know less than they do.

6. Keep translation apps as a last step

Translation apps are useful for checking a word, but if a child types the whole Korean sentence and copies the English, they learn very little. A good rule: try first, then check one or two words. The app is a hint, not a teacher.

7. Build English outside homework

Homework is only a small part of learning. Easy, enjoyable English, like picture books, songs, cartoons, and games, builds the ear and makes homework feel easier. Aim for input that your child understands most of and enjoys. Lots of very hard listening is tiring and doesn’t help much.

A simple routine

  1. Child explains the task.
  2. Child does it alone first.
  3. Parent reacts to the ideas (“Interesting! Tell me more.”).
  4. Parent points to one pattern; child finds and fixes the rest.
  5. Stop. Mistakes left over are for the teacher to see.
